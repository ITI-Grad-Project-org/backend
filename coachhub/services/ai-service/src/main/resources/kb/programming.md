# Programme design

## Choosing a training split

Split choice follows availability, not preference. Two days a week suits full-body sessions. Three days a week works well as full body or as push/pull/legs run once through. Four days suits an upper/lower split repeated twice. Five or six days suits push/pull/legs run twice, or a body-part split for advanced clients only. Each muscle group responds best to being trained roughly twice a week, so a split that hits everything once weekly is usually the weaker choice at the same total volume.

## Beginner push/pull/legs

A beginner push/pull/legs split trains each movement pattern about once per week across three sessions. Push covers chest, shoulders and triceps; pull covers back and biceps; legs covers quadriceps, hamstrings, glutes and calves. Three sessions suit new lifters building base strength before adding frequency. Keep it to five or six exercises per session and prioritise the compound lift first while the client is fresh.

## Weekly volume targets

Counting hard sets per muscle group per week is the most practical volume measure. Beginners progress on about 8 to 10 hard sets per muscle per week. Intermediates typically need 12 to 20. Beyond roughly 20 sets the returns flatten for most people and recovery becomes the limiting factor. Increase volume gradually across a block rather than starting at the top of the range, because there is nowhere to progress to from there.

## Deload weeks

Deload weeks every 4 to 8 weeks cut volume and intensity by roughly 40 to 60 percent to aid recovery and prevent overtraining. The usual approach is to keep the exercises and the weights but halve the number of working sets, or keep the sets and drop the load to about 60 percent. Signs a deload is overdue include stalled lifts across several exercises at once, disrupted sleep, elevated resting heart rate, joint aches and a drop in motivation.

## Periodisation in blocks

Block periodisation organises training into phases of about 4 to 8 weeks, each with one main emphasis: an accumulation block builds volume at moderate intensity, an intensification block raises load and cuts volume, and a deload or transition week separates them. It suits clients with a date to be ready for. For general population clients without a competition, a simpler repeating cycle of three progressive weeks followed by one lighter week is easier to run and nearly as effective.

## Exercise ordering within a session

Order exercises so that the highest-skill, highest-load work happens while the client is fresh. Compound barbell lifts first, then machine or dumbbell compounds, then isolation, then any conditioning. The exception is a deliberate pre-exhaust or a specific weak point being prioritised, which can be moved to the front on purpose. Placing heavy squats after twenty minutes of accessory work is the most common avoidable cause of a poor session.

## Warm-up structure

An effective warm-up is five to ten minutes and has three parts: general raise (light cardio to raise temperature), mobility for the joints the session will load, and ramp-up sets of the first exercise. Ramp-up sets progress from an empty bar toward the working weight in three to five steps without accumulating fatigue. Static stretching held for long durations before heavy lifting slightly reduces force output and is better placed after the session.

## Rest periods

Rest length follows the goal. Strength work at heavy loads needs 3 to 5 minutes between sets to restore force output. Hypertrophy work is usually well served by 90 seconds to 2 minutes — long enough that the next set is limited by the target muscle rather than by breathing. Isolation work on small muscles can be shorter, around 60 to 90 seconds. Cutting rest short on compound lifts turns a strength session into a conditioning session by accident.

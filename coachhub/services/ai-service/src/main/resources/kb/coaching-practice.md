# Coaching practice

## Client adherence

Adherence beats optimality. A programme a client completes 90 percent of will outperform a better programme they complete half of, so the first question about any plan is whether it fits the client's week. The strongest levers on adherence are session length under an hour, a schedule that matches their real availability, exercises they do not dread, and visible progress. When adherence drops, change the plan before questioning the client's commitment.

## Re-engaging a quiet client

A client who has not logged anything for a week is drifting, and the longer the silence runs the harder it is to recover. Reach out early with a specific, low-effort ask — one short session, or a single check-in question — rather than a general "how's it going". Find out whether the obstacle is time, motivation, confusion about the plan, or something outside training, because each has a different fix and only one of them is a programming problem.

## Setting expectations

Clients overestimate what is achievable in a month and underestimate what is achievable in a year. Set expectations in ranges and against timeframes: roughly 0.5 to 1 percent of bodyweight per week for fat loss, and meaningfully slower for muscle gain, especially past the first year of training. Being specific up front prevents the mid-programme disappointment that ends most coaching relationships.

## Progress tracking that is worth doing

Track a small number of things consistently rather than everything occasionally. Bodyweight taken at the same time of day, one or two circumference measurements, session logs with load and reps, and progress photos every four weeks cover almost every goal. Weight fluctuates by a kilogram or more day to day from food, salt and hydration, so judge the trend over two weeks, never a single reading.

## Reading adherence data

Session completion against what was scheduled tells you whether the plan is being followed; volume against what was prescribed tells you whether it is being followed properly. A client at 100 percent session completion but well under prescribed load is turning up and coasting, which needs a different conversation from one who is missing sessions entirely. Neither number means much without the other.

## Check-ins

A weekly check-in works best with a fixed short structure: what went well, what got in the way, the tracked numbers, and one thing to change. Keep it to something a client can complete in a few minutes, because a long form is the first thing to be skipped. The value is in the pattern across weeks rather than any single response — three consecutive check-ins mentioning poor sleep is a finding, one is noise.

## When to change a client's programme

Change a programme when it stops fitting the client, not on a fixed calendar. Real triggers are a change in availability, an injury, a stall that survived a deload, a goal change, or genuine boredom that is threatening adherence. Rotating exercises for novelty every few weeks prevents the client from progressing on anything long enough to see the progression, which is the point of the plan.

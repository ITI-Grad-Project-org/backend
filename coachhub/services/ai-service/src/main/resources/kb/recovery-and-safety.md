# Recovery, safety and working around limitations

## Sleep and recovery

Sleep is the single largest recovery variable a coach can influence. Most adults need 7 to 9 hours; consistently getting under 6 measurably reduces strength, work capacity and appetite regulation, and raises injury risk. When a client's performance drops without an obvious programming cause, ask about sleep before changing the programme. Practical levers are a consistent wake time, limiting caffeine after early afternoon, and a dark cool room.

## Managing muscle soreness

Delayed onset muscle soreness peaks around 24 to 72 hours after unfamiliar or high-volume work and is not a measure of session quality. A client can train a sore muscle group if the soreness is mild and does not alter movement; severe soreness that changes technique is a reason to reduce load or train something else. Soreness that persists beyond about five days, or that is sharply localised to a joint, should be treated as a possible injury rather than normal adaptation.

## Signs of overtraining

Genuine overtraining is rare; under-recovery is common. Warning signs include performance declining across several exercises at once, elevated resting heart rate, disturbed sleep, persistent joint pain, loss of appetite and loss of motivation. The response is to reduce volume first, then intensity, and to address sleep and food intake. Adding more training in response to these signs makes the situation worse.

## Training around an injury

Pain that is sharp, localised to a joint, or worsening session to session means stop the movement and refer to a medical professional. A coach's role is to train around a diagnosed injury, not to diagnose it. Working around one usually means keeping the pattern but changing the range, load or implement — for example replacing a barbell bench press with a neutral-grip dumbbell press for an irritated shoulder. Continue training everything unaffected; complete rest deconditions the client and rarely speeds recovery.

## Common contraindications to check

Before programming, check for cardiovascular conditions, uncontrolled high blood pressure, pregnancy, recent surgery, hernias, disc injuries and joint replacements. Any of these means the plan should be cleared with the client's doctor first. Uncontrolled high blood pressure in particular argues against heavy loading with breath-holding. Record what a client cannot do alongside what they can, because a plan built on an incomplete history is the plan most likely to hurt them.

## Older and deconditioned clients

Older and long-deconditioned clients respond well to resistance training and should not be limited to light weights on principle. What they do need is a longer ramp-in period, more attention to balance and joint tolerance, and machine or supported variations early on while control develops. Prioritise leg strength, hip hinge patterns and grip, since those track most closely with independence and fall risk.

## Menstrual cycle and training

Some clients report meaningful performance and recovery differences across the menstrual cycle, and others report none. The useful coaching approach is to track it alongside training data rather than to prescribe from a template: if a client consistently under-performs in a given phase, schedule lighter or technical work there and place heavier sessions where they perform best. Symptoms severe enough to prevent training are worth a medical conversation.

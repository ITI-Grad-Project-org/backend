# Progressive overload

## What progressive overload means

Progressive overload is the gradual increase of demand placed on the body so that it keeps adapting. Without it, training maintains fitness rather than building it. Load is only one lever: a coach can progress a client by adding weight, adding reps, adding sets, increasing range of motion, slowing the tempo, shortening rest periods, or improving technique at the same weight. Beginners should exhaust the simpler levers before adding weight.

## How fast to add weight

For most clients the safe rate is the smallest increment the equipment allows, applied when the client completes all prescribed reps on all sets with good form and two or more reps left in reserve. In practice that is roughly 2.5 kg per week on lower-body compound lifts and 1 to 2.5 kg per week on upper-body lifts for a novice, slowing to monthly or slower within the first year. Chasing a faster rate produces missed reps and technical breakdown, not faster progress.

## Double progression

Double progression is the most reliable scheme for intermediate clients. Prescribe a rep range rather than a fixed number — for example three sets of 8 to 12. The client stays at the same weight until they hit the top of the range on every set, then adds the smallest increment and drops back to the bottom of the range. It removes the guesswork about when to add load and it self-regulates around bad days.

## When progress stalls

A stall is not automatically a plateau. Check sleep, food intake, stress and total weekly volume before changing the programme, because all four affect performance faster than programming does. If those are sound and the client has missed the same target three sessions running, reduce the load by about 10 percent and build back up, or rotate to a close variation of the exercise. Repeating a failed session unchanged is the one response that reliably does not work.

## Reps in reserve and effort

Reps in reserve (RIR) describes how many more reps a client could have completed. RIR 2 means two left in the tank. Most productive hypertrophy work sits at RIR 0 to 3; strength work usually sits at RIR 2 to 4 to protect technique. Training every set to failure raises fatigue far more than it raises results and makes the next session worse, so reserve true failure for the last set of an isolation exercise.

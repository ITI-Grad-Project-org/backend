# Nutrition fundamentals

## Protein requirements

Protein for muscle gain is typically 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of bodyweight per day, spread across 3 to 5 meals. Going above roughly 2.2 g per kg has not been shown to add further benefit for most trainees. Clients in a calorie deficit sit at the higher end of the range, closer to 2.2 to 2.4 g per kg, because adequate protein is what preserves muscle while losing fat. For clients who find this hard to hit, a protein source at every meal is more practical advice than a daily total.

## Setting calories for fat loss

A deficit of roughly 15 to 25 percent below maintenance produces steady fat loss without wrecking training performance. That usually lands around 0.5 to 1.0 percent of bodyweight lost per week. Faster loss increasingly comes from muscle and is harder to sustain. Set the target from a two-week average of actual bodyweight rather than from a single weigh-in, and adjust only when the trend has clearly stalled for two weeks or more.

## Setting calories for muscle gain

A surplus of about 10 to 20 percent above maintenance supports muscle gain while limiting fat gain. For an untrained or returning client, aim for roughly 0.25 to 0.5 percent of bodyweight gained per week; for an experienced lifter, slower is realistic. Larger surpluses do not accelerate muscle growth beyond what training and protein allow — they mostly add fat, which then has to be dieted off.

## Carbohydrate and fat split

Once protein is set, the remaining calories divide between carbohydrate and fat largely by preference and training demand. A common starting point is 0.8 to 1.2 g of fat per kg of bodyweight, with carbohydrate taking the remainder. Clients doing high-volume or high-intensity training generally perform better with more carbohydrate. Neither macronutrient needs to be eliminated, and adherence matters more than the exact ratio.

## Meal timing

Total daily intake matters far more than timing for nearly every client. The one timing recommendation with reasonable support is spreading protein across three to five meals rather than concentrating it in one. The "anabolic window" immediately after training is much wider than it was once described — eating a protein-containing meal within a few hours either side of the session is sufficient. Timing advice should never displace effort spent on hitting daily totals.

## Hydration

A practical target is roughly 30 to 40 ml of fluid per kg of bodyweight per day, increased for hot climates and heavy sweating. Urine colour is a usable everyday check: pale straw suggests adequate hydration, dark yellow suggests more is needed. Losses of even 2 percent of bodyweight in fluid measurably reduce strength and endurance performance, so a client who trains dehydrated will under-perform for reasons that look like a programming problem.

## Fibre and micronutrients

Fibre intake of about 14 g per 1000 calories supports digestion and satiety, which makes a deficit easier to hold. Most of it should come from vegetables, fruit, legumes and whole grains rather than supplements. Clients in a prolonged deficit are the group most likely to fall short on micronutrients, so keep a wide range of whole foods in the plan rather than narrowing to a few "clean" items.

## Supplements worth discussing

Creatine monohydrate at 3 to 5 g daily is the best-supported supplement for strength and lean mass, and does not require loading. Caffeine improves training performance for most people at roughly 3 to 6 mg per kg taken about an hour before training. Whey or another protein powder is a convenience, not a requirement — it simply makes a daily protein target easier to reach. Most other products have weak evidence and are better spent on food.

# Movement patterns and technique

## The fundamental movement patterns

Most resistance programmes are built from six patterns: squat, hinge, lunge, push, pull and carry. A plan that covers all six trains the body evenly and exposes gaps quickly. Push and pull each divide into horizontal and vertical, so a thorough weekly plan includes a horizontal push (bench press), a vertical push (overhead press), a horizontal pull (row) and a vertical pull (pull-up or pulldown).

## Squat pattern cues

The squat loads the quadriceps, glutes and adductors. Set the feet about shoulder width with toes turned slightly out, brace the trunk before descending, and track the knees over the middle of the feet rather than letting them collapse inward. Descend to the depth the client can reach without the pelvis tucking under. Depth limited by ankle or hip mobility is a mobility problem to work on separately, not a cue to shout louder.

## Hinge pattern cues

The hinge — deadlift, Romanian deadlift, hip thrust — loads the hamstrings and glutes. The movement comes from pushing the hips backward with a mostly fixed knee angle, keeping the bar or weight close to the body, and finishing by driving the hips forward rather than leaning back. The most common fault is turning it into a squat by bending the knees early. Teach it with a light Romanian deadlift or a hip hinge against a wall before loading it.

## Pressing technique

In a bench press, keep the shoulder blades pulled back and down against the bench, lower the bar to the lower chest with the elbows at roughly 45 to 75 degrees from the torso rather than flared to 90, and press back over the shoulder joint. In an overhead press, brace the trunk and squeeze the glutes to stop the lower back from over-extending, and finish with the bar over the middle of the foot. Shoulder pain in pressing is most often a range or an elbow-angle problem before it is a load problem.

## Pulling technique

In a row, set the trunk, pull with the elbow rather than the hand, and stop when the upper arm is roughly in line with the torso — pulling further mostly rotates the shoulder forward. In a pull-up or pulldown, start from a full hang with the shoulder blades relaxed, then depress and retract them before the arms bend. Clients who cannot yet do a pull-up progress well through assisted variations and slow lowering from the top position.

## Tempo and range of motion

A controlled lowering phase of about two to three seconds with a full range of motion produces more adaptation per set than a fast partial one, and it is safer. Full range at a lighter weight beats partial range at a heavier one for nearly every general population goal. Deliberate partial ranges have specific uses for advanced trainees, but they should be a choice rather than what happens when the load is too heavy.

## Bracing and breathing

For most working sets, breathe in before the rep, brace the trunk as if about to be pushed, hold through the hardest part, and exhale as it eases. On heavy sets this held brace — the Valsalva manoeuvre — is what keeps the spine stable, but it also raises blood pressure sharply, so it is inappropriate for clients with cardiovascular conditions or uncontrolled hypertension. Those clients should exhale through the effort and stay at moderate loads.

## Machines versus free weights

Machines are not a lesser option. They reduce the skill and balance demand, make a muscle easier to isolate and easier to take close to failure safely without a spotter, and suit beginners, older clients and anyone training around an injury. Free weights train coordination and load the body in more transferable positions. Most good plans use both, weighted toward free weights for the main lifts and machines for accessory volume.
